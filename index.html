By type of role

Each group gets its own sick leave, stress share, pay, turnover and coaching uptake, so the ceiling on days avoided and the value of each day differ by group. When every group has the same uptake, splitting changes the total little, because the group figures add up to your company averages.

By type of job

Each job type values a sick day at the company's average daily employment cost times its own multiplier. Channel 1 is split over the job types by employees times multiplier. The multipliers come from the 3-day cost-of-absence column of Nicholson et al. 2006, table 4.

Where the cost sits, and which channel reaches it

Lost output at work is estimated at 2 days per stress-related sick day, half the ratio found by Stewart et al. 2003. It sizes the problem only and never enters the main return, because the trial evidence for it is weak.

What you will see in your own figures

Why these numbers

Where a study reports a 95% confidence interval, the expected value is its central estimate and the conservative and optimistic values are the two ends of the interval. The standard error behind each interval drives the simulation. Values without a confidence interval are marked.

Value of a sick day by type of job

Nicholson et al. 2006 asked managers what an absence in each job costs, as a multiple of the daily wage. The calculator uses the 3-day cost-of-absence multiplier from their table 4 for each job type you enter. Jobs not in the table, and employees not assigned to a job type, use 1.28. Rows for the jobs you entered are highlighted.

In the conservative outlook, the friction cost value of 0.80 for other jobs is scaled by the same ratio, so a job with a multiplier of 1.43 gets 0.80 × 1.43 ÷ 1.28. These are US jobs in the early 2000s, so the ranking between jobs is more reliable than the exact level.

Channels we considered, and why each is in or out

A channel enters the main return only when a controlled study shows it in euros an employer actually saves, and only once. Everything else is either counted elsewhere already, left to the company's own data, or left out.
